Opening

Before We Begin

What interests me is how a person can gain greater creative ability and decide where to use it.

AI has made some of these choices concrete. An idea can become a prototype sooner; an unusual personal need can have a tool of its own. As access to making expands, the next questions concern finding needs, judging results, arranging cooperation, and using time.

Greater ability can also change the rhythm of life. You may become more involved and more eager to learn, while finding more reasons to keep working. I want to consider creation and freedom together: making what you want and understanding what it costs.

The book has four parts. The first moves from personal making to time and markets. The second examines specific knowledge, judgment, and credibility through action. The third explores products, learning, and cooperation. The fourth returns to desire, interest, and choices about how to live. The emphasis is on concrete developments in later public material. Earlier concepts appear only as brief background where needed.

About the Voice of This Book

Creation and Freedom is an independently written Chinese work, translated here into English. It explores creation, judgment, and freedom through Naval Ravikant's public ideas from 2021–2026, using a first-person voice. Naval has neither authorized nor reviewed it. Personal experiences are identified and placed in context in the notes.

Web and EPUB notes provide sources and context. Deutsch and other contributors retain their attribution. The account of the material and writing method appears at the end.

Chapter 01 Turning Intention into Something Real

A Different Way to Begin

I can describe an application in ordinary language, ask the tool to draw up a plan and question me about missing details, then have it build the app. I can keep revising what I see running: this button is in the wrong place, this process takes too long, this feature feels different from what I imagined.

The change extends through the process of making. Tools are beginning to connect services, install dependencies, run programs, and check errors. An idea can travel this route to a prototype, with my feedback shaping it along the way.1

Taste used to stop at commentary much of the time: what was wrong with a product, how I wished it would change. Now some of those judgments can become things I can use. Making is moving closer to an intention the user can express.

Making an idea also changes it. Imagine a book-finding tool: you start with a field for the title, then discover at the bookstore counter that someone remembers only a blue cover and a picture of a ship. The prototype works, but you need to rethink how the search begins.

One User Can Be Enough

A workout tracker that fits your habits can exist for one person. How to enter sessions, which charts to see, what information to keep: the user knows these details best.2

A general-purpose app has to serve many people. An unusual preference might never reach its development schedule. Personal apps return that choice to the individual: a specific need and a useful result can be enough reason to try.

This leaves room for small needs. One person's way of keeping records, a small shop's working habits, a private routine can each give software a purpose. As making becomes cheaper, having few users becomes less of an obstacle to starting.

Revising Until It Feels Right

Making something alone offers another freedom: you can keep adjusting it according to a feeling you cannot yet fully explain. Move an icon left, move it back, try an unfamiliar interaction, then see whether you prefer using it.

Working with a team requires explaining those choices and respecting other people's time. Giving feedback to a tool reduces some of that communication and coordination. A personal vision can enter the prototype more fully, including the parts that seem odd at first.

I value that room to explore. Some work is initially appealing precisely because one person held on to a preference. Before trying it, you may struggle to give a reason everyone would accept for its existence.

The Work After the Prototype

Understanding how computers work still affects how far you can take a tool. Knowing how a program stores data, how a network connects, and how components fit together helps you make clearer requests and notice what has gone wrong.

Once you invite many people to depend on it, architecture, security, and scale become further work. A small fault you can work around privately might interrupt someone else's day. The prototype provides a beginning; a mature service also needs engineering skill and continued maintenance.3

I want the purpose of the work to determine the next step. Make it for yourself, improve it through use, then add the reliability and team it needs if it is worth serving more people. A personal app can already be a complete piece of work. Turning it into a business calls for another judgment.

Chapter 02 Taking Back Your Time

Whose Schedule Gains the Time?

Suppose you reduce a day's work to an hour. Where do the hours saved go? You might gain some free time, or receive another seven hours of tasks.

I place a high value on control of my time. Tools speed up execution; working arrangements determine who can use the time saved. When you answer for results and can arrange your own schedule, efficiency is more likely to reward you personally.4

This also changes how I think about productivity. Knowing why you are doing something and deciding what to address first gives you a chance to bring your full attention to the problem. Selling fixed hours often means handing over some of that control as well.

Autonomy Changes How Work Feels

When you truly work for yourself, the boundary between work and life can fade. You care about the problem, like the people working with you, and want to see the result yourself. The weekend arrives, and the ideas may keep coming.

That involvement is deeply appealing. A timetable can rarely command the same attention, and the work can become more enjoyable. This kind of self-directed effort can also happen in a small startup team.

Its cost also matters to me. When the work is yours, it is hard to switch it off. The stress of unmet goals follows you away from the desk. When you can set that stress aside for a while, the enjoyment in the work becomes easier to feel. That condition matters, and it is hard to meet.5

Once you have experienced this autonomy, clocking in, reporting, and waiting for instructions can feel especially restrictive. Who controls the schedule deserves consideration alongside pay when choosing work.

Doing Creates an Appetite for Learning

A difficult project can make abstract knowledge suddenly useful. You start seeking books, questioning models, listening to technical discussions, and asking people who have done related work. The obstacle in front of you gives learning somewhere to go.6

That motivation can sustain itself. You solve a little, encounter the next thing you do not understand, and work and learning begin to connect. Walking, talking, and experimenting become appealing because they help advance something you care about.

I want working conditions that allow this: some choice over the problem and room to learn as it develops. Tools expand what you can try; autonomy helps you use that ability on things you want to do.

You Still Choose Where the Hours Go

AI allows more tasks to be completed quickly. It can also draw someone into more intensive making. Feedback arrives so fast that it is easy to keep improving the next version, then try another idea.

Efficiency and autonomy therefore need separate consideration. How many tasks you finish is one question; whether you want to keep living this way is another. Tools can increase the available approaches. You still have to choose where the time goes.

I want uninterrupted time for a problem I have yet to understand, and some time when nobody is urging me onward.

Chapter 03 When Code Gets Cheaper, Who Owns the Value?

Features Become Easier to Build

Lower barriers to making software first expand the number of people who can try. Small needs that could never cover development costs may now be met. A theatre group's rehearsal arrangements or a researcher's search habits can each give rise to a tool.

I also want to know where the software's advantage lies. If it rests entirely on others being temporarily unable to build the same features, better coding tools will shorten that lead. Finding needs, reaching users, and connecting with existing workflows then carry more weight.

The engineers behind established applications have the same tools. They can fix problems faster, add features, and cover uses previously served by other software. New makers gain ability while existing entry points grow stronger.

Both Ends Could Grow

I expect we may see growth at both ends: a few mature applications serving broad needs, and many personal creations filling small uses. Software companies in the middle, whose advantage rests on easily reproduced features, could face pressure from both sides.7

The more abundant the supply, the more effort it takes to find the right tool. Large aggregators help people choose and gain value from making those connections. Amazon connects a long tail of sellers; YouTube connects a multitude of creators. These offer structures to think with.

Opportunities for small creations and the power of large entry points can grow together. It becomes easier for an individual to start making, while market returns may still concentrate in a few places. Understanding your position requires considering both changes.

Advantage Can Move Elsewhere

If a company’s entire advantage is “I can build software other people cannot,” I consider it uninvestable for venture capital. I would look further at hardware, network effects, and models themselves, asking which harder-to-reproduce conditions the software connects to.8

Cheaper software could also make hardware more useful. An otherwise good device used to be held back by clumsy control software. As coding tools and natural-language interfaces improve, more of its usefulness can be unlocked. Lamps, toys, and cameras offer possible directions to explore.

The structure of the model industry remains uncertain. Will training continue to concentrate? How far can open source go? How much privacy will users exchange for capability? I keep these questions open as I consider the evidence.

Can Contributors Share Ownership?

Beyond the ability to make lies a question of distribution: who owns the platform, who controls the data, and who can build on what already exists?

Web3 proposed several connected ideas: open code, users controlling their own data, and contributors sharing platform ownership. Open code lets applications combine like building blocks. Ownership arrangements seek to let those who build a network together share in its returns.9

This takes the question a step beyond enabling more people to publish. A platform can give creators an audience while retaining most of the economic benefit. Changing publishing tools and changing who receives the returns require different arrangements.

These are the questions I bring to new digital platforms: how work can be discovered, whether data can be taken elsewhere, how contributions are rewarded, and whether later makers can build on existing work. Tools widen access to creation; platform rules continue to shape who benefits.

Part 2 · Specific Knowledge and Credibility

Chapter 04 Discovering Your Specific Knowledge Through Action

Discovering Yourself in Difficult Situations

Specific knowledge includes a person's distinctive combination of abilities around particular problems. I am more interested in how to discover it: enter several difficult situations, observe how you respond, and listen to what people who actually work with you notice.10

Some abilities only become visible through use. Something that feels natural to you may remain difficult for the person beside you. Handling a hard problem, finding a customer, or helping people keep working together offers more concrete clues than describing yourself.

One entrepreneur's striking ability was the courage to tolerate rejection. He could make many sales calls, keep hearing no, and continue with a smile. When someone finally agreed, he had a place to begin learning and improving.11

This ability combines disposition, action, and belief in an outcome. It acquires practical value in work requiring repeated contact with customers. Discovering specific knowledge also means discovering the situations that can use it.

Choose What You Want to Keep Doing

Marketing has many forms: writing, making videos, talking face to face, organizing gatherings, and word of mouth. Each may work. Which one suits you also depends on whether you want to keep doing it.

Podcasting illustrates this well. Someone attracted by its reach but only reluctantly interested in conversation often struggles to continue after a few episodes. Listeners can sense that the host is waiting for the conversation to end.12

Enjoying it enough to keep talking without an audience gives sustained effort a chance. I would first observe the strength of that interest, then choose a business, role, or partner that lets daily work fit it.

Early attempts may involve many things you dislike and do poorly. That is part of their usefulness: gradually narrowing the field and finding the work in which you have more energy and more to offer.

Leave Time to Look Back

Iteration requires a moment to stop. Do something, examine the result, consider what worked and what needs changing, then try again. Learning happens in that reflection and adjustment.

Repeating the same approach can add hours while leaving understanding where it was. A useful attempt should change the next one: a different explanation, another kind of customer, a step removed because it serves no purpose.

I treat “What am I good at?” as a question whose answer can keep changing. The first answer may be broad. Actual results make it more precise: which problems, approached in which way, for which people do you handle especially well?

Chapter 05 Judgment Gives Leverage Its Direction

First Locate the Problem

Tools let a single decision affect more outcomes. I want to consider the choice of problem early: who it is for, what it solves, which conditions determine its usefulness. As execution speeds up, these choices carry more weight.13

A program can work tirelessly on the wrong problem. After seeing the result, you need to revisit the original request. Sometimes the implementation needs changing; sometimes your understanding of the problem does.

Compare Answers and Ask for Evidence

I would put the same question to several models, compare their answers, then ask for sources. Differences can suggest what to investigate. Verification still has to return to data and evidence.14

Models often produce fluent prose, and errors can hide in equally fluent explanations. Agreement between answers can be a lead worth checking. The answers may also share similar training material and biases.

This distinction matters especially when several agents work together. Ten instances of the same model still share that model and its training background. More instances can add computation and attempts; comparing models with different training backgrounds may offer another kind of difference. Their effects need separate examination.

A useful follow-up exposes a missing connection: “What supports this judgment?” “How does the conclusion change if this condition changes?” I want to see how the answer holds together before deciding whether to use it.

Watch the Local Patches

As a project grows, a model may lose its grasp of the whole. Its context cannot hold every detail, and it begins guessing, omitting things, or repeatedly patching the same error.

One especially troubling repair makes an error disappear by removing the use it affected. If you only check whether the program keeps running, this can slip through. You also need to return to the original requirements and confirm that the necessary features remain.

At this point, I need to examine the structure again: at what level does the problem occur, how do the parts connect, and will a local change push the difficulty elsewhere? When necessary, reorganizing the system may work better than another patch.

Judgment Grows from Particular Situations

Principles reveal their scope through actual work. The same advice about speed or caution may call for different actions in different circumstances. Looking back at results gradually teaches you when to use which judgment.

I use principles as aids to thinking, leaving room for actual conditions. A customer's needs, a technical limitation, or a team's abilities can each change the most suitable approach.

Through repeated use, judgment gradually becomes taste or intuition. You see a problem, and relevant experience comes to mind with it. That intuition has a history, and results must continue to revise it.15

Chapter 06 Credibility Means Being Willing to Lose a Sale

Explain What You Believe

I see selling as accurately conveying something I believe. First understand how it works and whom it suits, then explain that understanding to the other person. Knowledge and honesty give the explanation weight.

The people most worth working with often see through techniques. They need to trust that you understand the problem and will explain the important conditions. You can make something complex sound simple because you have thought through its relationships.

Give the Other Person Reasons to Trust

A trustworthy estate agent steers clients away from unsuitable deals, neighbourhoods, and houses. When the right opportunity appears, the recommendation carries weight.16

Concrete choices build that credibility. Disclosing a drawback may lose a sale, but the other person sees how you treat their needs. The client now has a concrete reason to trust the next recommendation.

Understanding begins with the other person's situation. Why do they believe their judgment? Which concern makes the proposal difficult to accept? Understanding those reasons makes the disagreement clearer.

I can acknowledge the part that makes sense, then explain my view. If disagreement remains, I can state it clearly. Honesty gives the conversation substance; consideration makes that substance easier to hear.

Reasons for Enthusiasm

I like explaining things to people who are interested. When a proposal fails to resonate, I can seek someone better suited to it, spending time on understanding and finding a fit.

Before presenting a company, I would first ask whether the business and team give me reason for excitement. Progress in the product, difficulties resolved, a more concrete prospect: these give an explanation something to stand on.

That enthusiasm comes from knowing the work. You understand what is worth doing, want to explain it, and acknowledge what remains unresolved. The other person encounters something you have already seen.

Fundraising also has practical timing requirements. Preparing early and beginning the search while cash still gives you room makes it easier to retain a choice of partners. Enthusiasm and preparation need to meet in the same work.

Choose Relationships You Want to Continue

Credibility pays forward into the next undertaking. Who wants to commit alongside you or entrust you with an important problem depends on how they understand the previous experience of working together.

Each deal becomes part of how people judge me. Winning a little more now can cost a partner in the next undertaking if it damages trust. Protecting myself when someone takes advantage of me also affects how future cooperation will work.17

Chapter 07 How a Good Product Holds Together

A Product Embodies an Understanding

A product embodies an understanding of a problem: what the user wants to accomplish, which conditions matter most, and how the parts should fit together. Features, form, and interactions turn that understanding into something usable.

David Deutsch argues that good explanations are hard to vary because their parts work together to account for a phenomenon. Vary one part arbitrarily, and the explanation loses some of its power. I apply the same criterion to a good product: its parts should possess such internal constraints.18

This asks more of you than “add features users like.” You need to find why the parts belong together. An improvement in one place should bring the whole object closer to its purpose.

How a Phone Holds Together

The iPhone’s screen, multitouch, embedded battery, size that fits in a pocket, and shape that feels smooth in the hand are organized around one thing: a personal computer you carry with you.

A larger screen makes reading more comfortable but changes portability and one-handed use. A bigger battery extends battery life while affecting thickness and weight. Each choice connects to others. It is hard to isolate one part and declare that more is always better.

The product becomes a stable whole. Components can improve and capabilities can grow while the basic form remains for a long time. Its parts have found a way to support one another.

Automotive aerodynamics provides another clear example. Once a design enters a wind tunnel, the air responds to its shape. Many cars have gradually acquired similar flowing lines because they share a need to reduce drag. Changing the shape carries a cost in efficiency.

Here, aesthetics connects to use. You can sense that something is unnecessary, that a transition is awkward, or that a whole has come together. Good design makes the result of repeated choices visible.

Simplicity Has to Be Made

Simplicity often arrives after iteration. At first, you do not know which part is truly useful. You have to build it and see how it works. As its purpose becomes clearer, parts that once seemed necessary can be removed.

Designing a vast, complex system first and then trying to make it work reliably creates many difficulties. Starting with a simple system that works, then improving it repeatedly, makes the results of each change easier to see.

I look back at what was added along the way. Some additions helped me understand the problem; others merely seemed necessary at that stage. As understanding grows, the product needs to be reorganized too.

I start by examining the requirement itself. Why is this step needed? Why must a user provide this information? If the reason for a requirement has disappeared, further optimization only makes you more proficient at doing something unnecessary.19

Making these trade-offs requires someone who understands the whole problem. Different people can design the components, but someone must still know: why is this part here, and what happens to the others if it is removed? Understanding the whole gives local improvements direction.

Letting Reality Respond

A product puts an understanding to the test of reality. You can check whether a rocket launches, a drone flies, or a printer meets its targets for precision, time, and cost.

Such feedback is precious. Human appraisals often mix in politeness, encouragement, and relationships. The physical world reveals directly where your understanding has failed. The market also asks a specific question: will someone pay for it?20

Chapter 08 Making Understanding Your Own

A Paragraph Can Last a Long Time

I like writing with a high density of thought. You finish a paragraph and spend the next hour thinking about it. A good author has done the cutting for the reader, putting a set of relationships into a concise expression that the reader can continue to unfold.21

This kind of reading changes with experience. On a first reading, you understand part of a book. Returning later, other passages become clear. The words remain where they were; the questions you bring back have changed.

Deutsch’s books particularly reward this approach. A concept connects to knowledge, wealth, products, and everyday choices. You first understand it in one place, then gradually discover what it illuminates elsewhere. Those connections give the work its density.

On any reading, it is enough to take in what you are ready to understand. Become interested in a passage, stop to investigate, think, or try something, then return. Understanding will keep growing.

Bringing Questions to the Tool

AI is like a calculator for reading and writing, extending the ability to work with words. You can ask it to explain a concept, put it differently, or provide an example, then continue along the points you have not understood.22

I can gain some understanding from a book, add more by rereading and listening to related discussions, then ask a model questions and dig into one point. Along this path, the tool becomes another connection between the reader and knowledge.

The most valuable moment is when you can identify where you are stuck. Why does this conclusion follow from that premise? Would it still hold if one condition changed? Where, precisely, do two apparently similar claims differ? The more specific the question, the more easily the answer becomes part of your understanding.

Sometimes you understand A and understand B but cannot see how they connect. A model can adjust the level of explanation, try a diagram or analogy, and continue from what you already know. When the explanation meets you at the edge of your learning, concepts that previously stood apart have a chance to connect.23

Depth Grows from the Problem at Hand

Studying a problem often brings you to further problems. You can follow them deeper, or use an existing explanation for now and continue the work in front of you. The depth depends on what you want to understand at this moment.

Deutsch starts with visible problems, favouring ones whose exploration remains enjoyable even if a solution proves elusive. I want to learn in that direction: knowing why I need the knowledge and wanting to spend time understanding it.24

A problem that truly troubles you organizes your attention. You remember the differences between explanations and become more willing to seek counterexamples. Concepts encountered in reading finally have something to address.

Older Books for Human Nature, the Frontier for Technology

Choose newer or older sources according to the question you are asking. For human nature, you can read books that have stood the test of time. For changing technologies, knowledge at the frontier is often more useful and becomes obsolete more quickly.25

Schopenhauer’s short essays on human nature retain their force, while judgments about the science, medicine, and politics of his time need to be placed back in their period. Even within one author’s work, some parts can inspire for a long time while others have become outdated.

I let the purpose determine how I read. To understand a recurring question about human nature, I can slow down and reread. To address a technical difficulty, I seek sufficiently recent material. The problem guides both the age of the source and the pace of reading.

Part 3 · Understanding and Choice

Chapter 09 Choose a Difficulty You Are Willing to Take On

Breaking a Title into Daily Work

When choosing work, we often first see its appeal: entrepreneurship offers autonomy, management offers influence, and creative work offers expression. What each day requires deserves a separate look.

I prefer to understand a role through its responsibilities. Would you rather keep revising something you made or continually coordinate a group? Are you more comfortable with uncertain technology or uncertain people? They require different kinds of patience.

A CEO's responsibilities can be separated into several jobs: raise funds and keep cash available, set and communicate strategy, and recruit and retain talent. A founder may also lead product or sales. Enjoying one part needs to be considered alongside willingness to keep doing the others.26

Someone can provide a company's product vision while leaving organizational leadership to a more suitable person. Separating the title makes the work clearer and gives different people room to contribute.

Help People Want to Do the Work

Leading a team requires understanding why people want to commit. A task may serve the company, but it also needs a connection to members' abilities and personal goals.

I listen carefully to what someone wants, then consider which work would make them want to use their abilities. When goals overlap, explaining direction, providing what people need, and removing obstacles become concrete work.

This makes demands on the leader too. You need to believe in the undertaking, explain why it deserves effort, and let the team see actual progress. A fixed set of lines is unlikely to sustain someone else's motivation for long.

The Freedom and Demands of a Small Team

Individual tools keep improving, while small teams can still connect different kinds of knowledge. I value the smallest team capable of doing the job: each person takes on an important part and can trust the others' work.

The stag hunt illustrates this cooperation: alone, you can catch only smaller prey; together, you have a chance at a larger reward. Trust makes people willing to commit and helps that commitment endure.27

A flat team organized this way requires members who can judge independently and find the people they need to speak with, while someone retains an understanding of the whole. Fewer layers allow direct contact and hand some coordination work to each member.

Tools can help people cross a short distance between disciplines. A hardware specialist can write software for an initial test; a model researcher can build an experimental harness. Once the preliminary work exists, the handover becomes more concrete. Production delivery may still need the relevant specialists to take over.

Choose Cooperation That Fits

I consider what an organizational arrangement actually demands. Some people enjoy finding problems and collaborators for themselves; others prefer clear duties and reporting relationships. Choosing a team means choosing how you will repeatedly encounter difficulties.

At one stage, you may enjoy making things alone; later, helping a group accomplish something may appeal more.

Part 4 · Desire and Freedom

Chapter 10 How Desire Claims Your Time

Peers and an Audience

I pay attention to comparisons with peers. Where standing among peers is still fluid, one person's advance can make the others reassess their position.

An audience intensifies this. An argument begins to concern who gained the upper hand, who won approval, and who needs to save face as well as its actual subject. Attention gradually moves toward public standing.28

A platform such as Twitter brings these conditions together. People stand like peers on the same field before a global audience. Likes, reposts, and follower counts leave a score for every exchange.29

Scores Can Redirect Attention

If I make these scores the measure of my work, I will increasingly care about what provokes a reaction. A question I have yet to understand may be set aside because it is hard to express. A low-value argument can feel urgent because many people are watching.

The change is often subtle. At first, I only want to explain a judgment. Later, I keep returning to see how others respond. The point has been made; my attention remains caught in the ranking.

At that point, I can pause and ask: will the next action help me understand something, or keep the public comparison going? Recognizing the activity makes it easier to decide how much time to give it.

A Wish Can Interrupt Contentment

Contentment may be a spell of ease with life as it is. A meal, a conversation, or a piece of work just finished can make the present feel sufficient for a while.30

A new wish changes that ease. You become concerned with something you do not yet have, wait to obtain it, then return to calm. The wish fading can also allow the same calm to reappear.

Both ways of returning deserve attention. Some things are worth pursuing; some longings lose their importance after a while. Seeing a wish arrive and pass helps me understand what it has occupied.

Leave Room for the Present

Contentment is fragile, which gives us reason to recognize it when it appears. Gratitude without a special reason, a moment of beauty simply felt, closeness to someone without an added demand: these can happen on an ordinary day.

I can pursue a goal seriously while noticing what is already here. To judge how much time a wish deserves, I consider both what it might bring and which parts of life it keeps waiting.

Part 4 · Desire and Freedom

Chapter 11 Can Creativity and Happiness Coexist?

A Creator Who Dares to Be Happy

Few creative people dare to be happy. If I become content, will I keep making things? Is a sense of something missing what keeps me moving?31

I want to keep that question open while observing another source of motivation: a strong interest in something. A problem can draw you in on its own. Understanding it and making something from it already give you reasons to act.

Feed Intellectual Obsessions

An intellectual interest can occupy a large share of attention. A technology, a field of knowledge, or a new way of practising makes you want to engage intensely, keep questioning, and spend time understanding the details.

When an intellectual interest takes hold, I feed it and let a period of life revolve around it. Its intensity will eventually pass, but a substantial part of what I learn stays with me, entering my abilities, habits, and thinking.32

When a new interest arrives, the previous learning becomes something you can carry into it. One life can contain several such periods. Each leaves you with more knowledge and more to explore.

Making Offers Real Feedback

AI coding gives this involvement a quick response. Describe an idea, see it run, change something, see the difference. Making keeps offering another thing to try.

It resembles a game's challenge adjusting to your ability, while leaving the result in the real world. A tool that once existed only in your head begins solving a problem of your own. You can choose the next goal and let it expand with the work.33

Knowing clearly what you want strengthens the experience. The changes in front of you have direction. You can judge whether they bring the tool closer to what you want to use or simply add features.

How Interest Enters a Life

Intense involvement also redistributes time. Evenings once spent reading, browsing, or playing may gradually go toward making. The enjoyment is real, and so is the time it occupies.34

I want to give a worthwhile interest room to develop fully. When it passes, what I have learned becomes a starting point for the next part of life.

Part 4 · Desire and Freedom

Chapter 12 What Kind of Day Should Wealth Make Possible?

Success Still Leaves a Choice

Once a way of making money succeeds, the obvious next step is to expand it. A larger fund, more resources, higher income all have familiar arguments in their favour. Continuing what you already do well also feels secure.

I still ask whether I want to give the next part of my life to it. An approach can keep producing returns while personal interest has moved elsewhere. Repeating success and starting a new problem each occupy real time.35

Being willing to forgo some income lets other ways of living have a chance. That choice requires financial room and an acknowledgment: ranking everything by maximum income could lead me to choose differently.

Cooperation Constrains the Future

A contract establishes how people will work together. Both sides give up some options for the possibility of creating more together. Deciding how to cooperate also determines which paths remain open.

Some relationships are easy to end; others last a long time. Once an investor enters a company, board seats, voting rights, and other terms affect later decisions. These consequences deserve attention before signing.

I want room to walk away. Preparing funding and partnerships in advance can reduce the pressure to accept something at the last minute. When compromise is necessary, I still need to understand what I am giving up and whether the path is worthwhile.

Save Time for Larger Possibilities

A small disagreement can occupy a long time. You keep replaying what you deserve, how to prove the other person wrong, and what to say next. The amount may be limited while the matter keeps returning to your life.

I would rather consider what continued cooperation can create and what leaving can give back. Some principles deserve defending; some outcomes deserve accepting so that time can go toward a new attempt. Whether I can sleep peacefully belongs in that judgment too.

Peace has a practical use here: ending an argument and becoming interested in the next undertaking. The choices wealth provides need to be exercised in these decisions.

Several Lives Within One

I am willing to begin again when a new problem genuinely interests me, and to learn after becoming skilled at something else. The choice shows up in each day: whom I work with, what I want to do on waking, and whether I can rest peacefully at night.

Afterword: Bringing Understanding Back to Your Life

I want to take these questions back into life: can I begin making what I want, does the work in front of me fit, how much time is a partnership worth, and what do I want to continue after success?

I have to answer these questions in the circumstances of my own life. An idea from a conversation offers a way of looking; my work, companions, and daily experience test whether it is useful.

Creation makes some choices possible. How you use those choices gradually shapes the life you lead.

A Note on the Sources

Scope and Writing

This book organizes Naval Ravikant's public statements from 2021 through 2026 around creation, judgment, and freedom. The research archive covers material collected through October 1, 2026. The emphasis is on this period's exploration of personal making, market structure, action, products, learning, and choices about life. A later publication may also continue an earlier idea.

The independently written first-person narrative combines summaries, explanations, and explicitly hypothetical examples. The notes identify the sources and context of personal experiences. Ideas expressed by Deutsch and other participants retain their attribution. Naval has neither authorized nor reviewed the book.

Verification relied mainly on locally archived texts, official tweet responses, and captions. The audio and video has not all been checked sentence by sentence. Comparison with the original Almanack is partial, and coverage of 2026 still has gaps.

Notes and References

Notes provide original references, personal context, and relevant background. Web notes open as needed; the EPUB provides chapter notes with return links. The PDF omits notes and note numbers by default, retaining the main text and this account of its sources. The text is the same across all three formats.

Discussions of market structure, model capabilities, and investment appeal retain the original conversations' timing, predictions, and open questions. Clips and the full transcript of one conversation count as one source. A page's publication date and any confirmed recording date are recorded separately. Readers needing a verbatim quotation can follow the notes to the original expression.

Main Sources

The 2026 conversations A Motorcycle for the Mind and A Return to Code discuss making through natural language, personal applications, model use, and the engineering conditions of prototypes. “Nothing Ever Happens” Is Over addresses market structure, hardware, and cooperation across disciplines in small teams. Sell the Truth supplies concrete contexts for credibility, understanding others, intellectual interests, contracts, and choices about life.

The 2025 conversation In the Arena connects specific knowledge discovered through action, suitable ways of working, iteration, dense reading, and product design. These discussions form an important thread through the book.

The Deutsch Files I, published in 2024, includes Deutsch's answers about starting with particular problems and valuing the enjoyment of exploration. These ideas retain his attribution. The relevant notes link to original posts on products, judgment, desire, and the 2025 reading post.

The 2021 conversation with Tim Ferriss, Chris Dixon, and Naval provides the proposed Web3 arrangements for open code, data, and platform ownership. This book uses Naval's passages. The Matt Mochary interview concerns CEO responsibilities and roles. The 2021 posts on public audiences and creative people's happiness, the 2022 post on control of time, and the 2023 reading-and-writing calculator post are read alongside the longer sources.

Relationship to the Earlier Book

The earlier Almanack chapters on leverage, specific knowledge, and happiness are gathered here as comparative background. Happiness, compiled and published in 2021, contains earlier conversations and is treated as background too. This edition compresses the recaps of those sources. The chapter notes focus on the material used from 2021 through 2026.
